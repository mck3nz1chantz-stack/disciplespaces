# Beginner study — what the guides say

**Status:** Partial. A deep-research pass ran on 2026-10-22. This note uses only the pages opened again after that pass. Claims from pages that were not re-opened are listed under “Not used,” and they are not requirements.
**Date:** 2026-10-22
**App:** DiscipleSpaces. This note does not approve a feature and does not change the app.

The question was what disciple-makers and beginner guides say a learner needs in order to stay oriented, without a complicated system. Four questions. Published pages and one study-method essay are separated below from guesses.

## 1. How a beginner meets a reference

**What the pages say.** A reference is a book, a chapter, and a verse. Written down, the chapter and the verse are usually split by a colon. A period shows up sometimes. The way back is that reference. Page numbers change from Bible to Bible, so a page number is not a stable address.

Finding it is a skill people are embarrassed not to have. Teach it to the whole group. The table of contents is the legitimate way to find a book. Memorizing all 66 books is more than a new reader needs. In an app, typing the reference is the direct way in. The chapter number is the larger one. The numbers after the colon are the verses.

A spoken reference can be unclear. “First Corinthians, chapter 13, verse 4” and “4 to 6” are the same kind of address. Asking the speaker to repeat it is the recommended fix. “John” is easy to confuse with 1 John, 2 John, and 3 John.

Sources: [Finding the Reference](https://answersingenesis.org/church-edition/blog/2023/08/21/finding-reference/) (Answers in Genesis, Evonne Krell, 21 August 2023). [Find A Scripture](https://theorchardchurch.net/assets/articles/bible_basics_4_find_a_scripture/) (The Orchard Church, Doug Ellis, 23 July 2024).

**What they do not support.** A full chart of every mark. The pages opened here do not define a comma, a semicolon, “ff,” or a letter on a verse such as 16a. They do not say the app should explain what John 3:16 *means* as theology. They explain how to find the verse.

One children’s section in the Answers in Genesis article also uses speed races (“sword drills”). That is a teaching game. It is not a requirement for a personal study app, and it fights this app’s existing refusal of scores.

## 2. Tying one verse to another

**What the pages say.** Look at what the passage says before you decide what it means. Skipping that observation leaves the meaning resting on feelings, opinion, or what someone else said. Comparing the passage with other passages is called the most important help for interpretation: Scripture read alongside Scripture.

A cross-reference is another verse on the same person, topic, or wording. The places these writers tell you to get one are the margin or footnote, a concordance, or an app or site that shows them as soon as you click (they name Blue Letter Bible, Bible Gateway, Logos, openbible.info, and the Treasury of Scripture Knowledge). After you open one, write what you notice and ask who, what, when, where, and why, so the link stays in its own passage. Some suggested links fit better than others.

Doing without any comparison tempts a private conclusion. Consulting a list is not the same thing as proof-texting. Proof-texting, in the essay that draws this line, is a list chosen to defend a point while leaving out verses that would challenge it. A cross-reference collection, in print or on a screen, is a tool that writer recommends, then still expects you to test.

Sources: [How to Study Your Bible](https://www.precept.org/2023/03/how-to-study-your-bible/) (Precept, 14 March 2023). [What are cross-references?](https://preceptireland.org/articles/2025/10/16/what-are-cross-references) (Precept Ireland, David Wilson, 16 October 2025). [How to do an Inductive Bible Study: Cross-References](https://biblebee.org/htdibscr/) (National Bible Bee, Hannah Kohner, 16 June 2021). [Cross Reference Bible Study is not the same as Proof-texting](https://www.realbiblestudy.com/?p=3982) (Real Bible Study, Jerry, 10 May 2024). The last one is one writer’s essay, not a church curriculum.

**What they do not support.** A rule that the only good link is one the learner invents, with printed and automatic lists treated as a danger. These pages tell you to use those lists. They also do not say an automatic list may be obeyed as if it were the text. They do not pick one product this app must ship. They do not say a single hand-typed link is the whole method. One link the learner adds is a small start. A library is a larger, separate decision.

## 3. Reviewing saved verses

**What the pages say.** Where review is actually described, it is Scripture memory, not a pile of notes. The Navigators’ Topical Memory System is one curated list, grouped by topic. They tell you to keep a picture of that list on your phone, to read the paragraph before and after the verse, and to spend about ten minutes a week checking in with a friend.

That page does not split the list into “this group’s verses” and “a total across every study.” It does not mention streaks, point scores, or guilt.

Source: [How to Memorize Scripture](https://www.navigators.org/resource/how-to-memorize-scripture/) (The Navigators). The downloadable tool linked from that page is the same system.

**What they do not support.** A published way to review verses saved inside one group study and again as a total across every study, without streaks, scores, or guilt. The memory advice nearby is a partner, a short weekly check, and the surrounding paragraph. It is not a design for this phone’s notes. Copying a 100-day memory streak, or a leader’s commendation for a perfect recitation, into this app is not supported by the page opened here, and it would break the lock against streaks and scores.

The deep-research run also named the Navigators 2:7 leader guides and Andrew Davis’s extended-memory booklet. Those PDFs were not re-opened for this note. Their schedules are not requirements.

## 4. Which paper habits to keep

**What the pages say.**

- One passage for the whole week, worked in more than one sitting: daily questions, then the group, then teaching, then notes. Bring a pen and paper or a device, and a Bible or a Bible app. Not finishing is allowed. Bible Study Fellowship’s own lessons use the NIV. They tell you to bring the translation you have. Source: [Getting Started at BSF](https://www.bsfinternational.org/bsf/what-to-expect).
- A bare passage, titles and study notes stripped off, so the reader does the work. InterVarsity prefers paper for that habit because a phone is where people read casually. They still tell you to make the page from a Bible website. Source: [What is a Manuscript?](https://library.intervarsity.org/library/what-manuscript) (InterVarsity).
- One topical verse list you can carry. A phone photo of the list is explicitly allowed. Pocket cards are offered as a product, not as the only form. Source: the Navigators page above.
- Writing while you read, in a notebook or journal, so you notice the passage. Source: the Precept page above, under Bible journaling.
- Leaving the phone outside the room when notifications pull you away. Typing a reference on a phone is still described as the fast path. Source: the Orchard article above.

**What they do not support.** One notebook per group, as a habit these writers are trying to keep or as a habit they call chaotic. Sticky notes, either way. A rule that every app is the problem. The problems they do name are a casual skim in place of the passage, study notes that do the reader’s work, a cross-reference list used without its surrounding passage, and a phone that interrupts.

This app already keeps a separate study per group. The sources neither require that split nor forbid it. They do not say to merge every note into one pile.

## Questions only a local mentor can answer

The pages do not answer these. Do not fill them in from a guess.

1. Besides book, chapter, and verse, which marks should this group learn first: a through-span (“16 to 18”), a comma, a semicolon, “ff,” or a letter on part of a verse?
2. If this group uses a ready-made cross-reference list, which one, and who checks that a suggested verse still fits?
3. When someone here saves a verse, are they memorizing it with a partner on a schedule, or only keeping a reference they can open again? How often, and when is an old passage let go?
4. For this group, which habit matters: the weekly passage, a bare page with no study notes, a notebook written while reading, or one topical list? Do Wednesday and Friday stay separate notebooks?
5. Should a typed “John” ever warn that 1 John, 2 John, and 3 John are different books, or is the Gospel always the right landing?
